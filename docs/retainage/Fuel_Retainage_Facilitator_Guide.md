# Facilitator Guide -- Fuel Usage & Shipper Retainage Onboarding
### How to run the team onboarding session
*Companion to the onboarding deck (the slides you present) and the accounting playbook (the reference the team keeps).*

**One session goal, in one line:** everyone leaves able to say why we keep fuel -- and why we can't profit from it.

---

## 1. About this guide
This guide is for whoever runs the onboarding session. It turns the slide deck into a facilitated conversation: what to say on each slide, what to ask, what confusion to expect, and a short exercise that ties the ideas back to the workbooks the team already uses.

> **The single most important instruction:** Teach the principle first, then map it to the workbook -- **never the other way around.** The team's workbooks are rich and detailed, and that detail is exactly what has been hiding a simple idea. Your job is to strip the idea bare, make it stick, and only then point at a couple of columns in the sheet. If you open the workbook first, you lose them.

Success is not that they can navigate a tab or a formula. Success is that they can explain, in their own words, why the pipeline keeps fuel and why keeping too much is a problem. The capability they already have in the workbooks becomes far more effective once that idea is solid.

---

## 2. Before you facilitate
| | |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Operational accounting team members who work the fuel workbooks. No regulatory or modeling background assumed. |
| **Duration** | ~50 minutes full. Short version ~30 min (slides 1, 3, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15). Add 10 min for the workbook exercise. |
| **Materials** | The deck projected; the playbook as a handout; one representative workbook open but kept minimized until the exercise. |
| **Format** | Works live or virtual. If virtual, plan one deliberate pause on each "Ask the room" prompt -- silence online feels longer; wait it out. |
| **Your one job** | Every attendee leaves able to say the north-star sentence and explain over- versus under-recovery in plain words. |

## 4. Five facilitation principles
1. **Lead with the idea, not the numbers.** Every segment starts from a plain sentence, not a column of data.
2. **Make them say it back.** Understanding shows up in their words, not your slides. Ask people to restate.
3. **Park the mechanics.** When someone asks "how do I do that in the sheet," capture it on a visible list for a follow-up working session. Don't let one workbook question pull the room into the weeds.
4. **Repeat the north star at least three times** -- open, middle, and close. Repetition is the whole strategy.
5. **Normalize the confusion.** Say plainly: the complexity has been hiding a simple idea; struggling with it is not a personal failing.

## 5. Slide-by-slide notes

**Slides 1-3**
- *Key message:* The north star -- we set our fuel rate, but we can't profit from fuel.
- *Say it like:* Everything today comes back to one sentence. Read it, then pause and let it sit.
- *Ask the room:* Before we start -- in a word, what is fuel retainage? (Collect a few guesses; don't correct yet.)
- *Watch for:* People wanting to jump to the workbook. Hold them -- idea first.

**Slides 4-5**
- *Key message:* We keep a small slice of each shipper's gas, in-kind, to cover fuel burned and gas lost.
- *Say it like:* We burn gas to run compressors and lose a little moving it. Instead of buying that gas, we keep a sliver of the shipper's.
- *Ask the room:* Why gas and not cash? (It's taken in-kind, a slice of what they hand us to move.)
- *Watch for:* The idea that this is a "fee" or "charge" for profit. It's reimbursement for a real cost.

**Slides 6-7**
- *Key message:* Fuel is a cost to recover, not a margin to earn. Keep more -> owe it back; keep less -> may collect.
- *Say it like:* If we keep more gas than we burn, that surplus isn't ours -- we owe it back. Keep less, and we may recover it.
- *Ask the room:* If we were super efficient and used way less fuel, do we get to keep the extra gas we retained? (No -- see slide 7.)
- *Watch for:* The instinct that efficiency = keeping the surplus. Efficiency shows up by lowering the rate, not by pocketing gas.

**Slide 8**
- *Key message:* Two steps -- estimate early for timeliness, then true up to measured actuals.
- *Say it like:* First we estimate so the books aren't blank. Then, when the real measured numbers land, we replace the estimate and record the difference.
- *Ask the room:* Which of these two numbers is the "truth" -- the estimate or the measured actual? Why?
- *Watch for:* Treating the estimate as final. The estimate is for speed; the measured true-up makes it right.

**Slides 9-10**
- *Key message:* Over-recovery is a liability (owe back); under-recovery is an asset (may collect). Fuel nets to zero in earnings.
- *Say it like:* We kept 20,000, used 19,200 -- 800 units over. That 800 isn't profit; it's owed back. It sits on the balance sheet, not in earnings.
- *Ask the room:* Flip it: if we'd used 20,800, what would we call it, and what could we do about it?
- *Watch for:* "Over-recovery is good news / more money." Reframe: it's a debt, and a growing one is a problem.

**Slides 11-12**
- *Key message:* Close the loop -- reset the go-forward rate and return/collect the accumulated balance. Then check what "good" looks like.
- *Say it like:* Measuring the gap isn't enough -- we move the rate so what we keep ~ what we use, and give back or collect the running balance.
- *Ask the room:* Look at the five "good" checks -- which one is hardest for us to hit today?
- *Watch for:* Vague agreement. Push for a specific answer; it reveals where the real gap is.

**Slide 13 + exercise**
- *Key message:* Your work is the control that keeps fuel honest and defensible.
- *Say it like:* Get this right and we're compliant and fair; let a surplus quietly build and it's exactly what gets challenged. You are that control.
- *Ask the room:* (Run the workbook exercise in Section 6 here.)
- *Watch for:* The pull into formulas during the exercise. You only need two numbers -- resist the deep dive.

**Slides 14-15**
- *Key message:* Recap terms; close on the north star.
- *Say it like:* Quick vocabulary, then the one line to remember: set the rate to break even on fuel, never to profit from it.
- *Ask the room:* Say the north star back to me in your own words. (Go around; make everyone do it.)
- *Watch for:* Letting people leave silent. The restatement is the point -- don't skip it to save two minutes.

## 6. The workbook tie-in exercise
Optional but strongly recommended -- this is where the principle "calcifies" onto the tool they already use. Budget 10 minutes.

**Setup:** Open one representative workbook where the group can see it. You need exactly two numbers for one period: the gas retained from shippers, and the gas actually used and lost. Nothing else.

**Run it:**
1. Point to the two numbers. Don't explain how they're calculated -- just locate them.
2. Subtract: retained minus used/lost. Positive or negative?
3. Ask: over-recovered or under-recovered? Say it in plain words.
4. Ask: is that a liability we owe back, or an asset we might collect?
5. Ask: what should happen next -- does the rate hold or move, and is a balance returned or collected?

> **Facilitator caution -- resist the dive.** The moment someone opens a formula or jumps tabs, you've lost the thread. You need two numbers and a direction. If methodology questions come up -- how the estimate is built, how the rate is set -- write them on the parking list and schedule a separate working session. That is a different meeting.

## 7. Common misconceptions and how to correct them
| What you'll hear | How to correct it |
|---|---|
| "The extra gas we keep is revenue / margin." | It's owed back. Over-recovery is a liability to shippers, not income. |
| "If we under-recover, we just eat the cost." | Not necessarily -- it can be a recoverable asset if we're likely to collect it later. |
| "The workbook's estimated number is the answer." | That's the estimate. The truth is the measured actual, and we true up to it. |
| "Fuel makes money for the pipeline." | We earn a return on the pipe we built -- never on the fuel we retain. |
| "Being precise means the estimate must be perfect." | No -- estimate for speed. Precision comes from the measured true-up. |
| "A surplus just carries forward, no big deal." | A steadily growing surplus is exactly what regulators and shippers challenge. |

---

## 8. Checks for understanding
Use these as exit questions. If the room answers all four cleanly, the session worked.
1. Say the north star in your own words.
2. We kept 20,000 and used 19,200 -- over or under, what do we owe, and where does it sit?
3. What's the difference between the estimate step and the true-up step?
4. If we keep over-collecting month after month, what has to change?

---

## 9. Make it stick
- Within a week, run a short working session mapping the two steps onto the actual workbook -- that's where capability compounds.
- Put the north-star sentence somewhere the team sees it regularly.
- Revisit the "what good looks like" checklist at a real month-end, against real numbers.
- Point people to the playbook for depth; keep this guide and the deck as the teaching layer.
- Clear the parking list from the exercise -- unanswered mechanics questions erode the confidence you just built.

---

## 10. Quick answers to tough questions
| Question | Plain answer |
|---|---|
| "Why can't we keep the surplus if we were efficient?" | When we adjust the rate to track cost, the regulator's deal is: no surplus, ever. Efficiency shows up by lowering the rate -- not by keeping gas. |
| "Isn't some over/under unavoidable?" | Yes. That's exactly why the true-up and rate resets exist -- to push the running balance back toward zero over time. |
| "How precise does the estimate have to be?" | Good enough to be timely. The measured true-up is what makes the period right. |
| "Where does this hit the P&L?" | On net, it shouldn't. Over/under sits on the balance sheet and clears through the rate. |
| "Who says we can't profit from fuel?" | Our regulator, FERC. The playbook has the specifics; you don't need them to run this session. |

*Keep the playbook within reach for anyone who wants the regulatory and accounting detail behind these answers.*
