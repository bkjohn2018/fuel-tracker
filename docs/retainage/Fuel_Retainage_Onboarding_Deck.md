### Slide 2 -- Start here: the what and the why
- **What we're doing:** Accounting for the small amount of gas we keep from shippers to run the pipeline.
- **Why it matters:** A regulator lets us set the rate -- but only enough to cover our fuel, never to profit from it.
- **What you'll leave with:** The vocabulary and the logic to handle fuel retainage with confidence.

> **Notes:** We are not training on a tool today. We're building the mental model. Everything downstream -- entries, filings, systems -- makes sense only once these principles click.

---

### Slide 3 -- The north star
> **We can set our own fuel rate. We just can't profit from fuel.**
> Every rule and every entry in this guide comes back to this one idea.

> **Notes:** This is the anchor. Read it out loud. The regulator (FERC) gives us freedom to set our fuel rate, but attaches one condition: fuel is a cost to recover, not a source of earnings. Keep coming back to this line.

---

### Slide 4 -- What is fuel retainage?
To move a shipper's gas across the pipeline, we burn some gas in our compressors, and a little is lost along the way. Instead of buying that gas, we keep a small percentage of every shipper's gas in-kind. That slice is fuel retainage.

`100% shipper's gas in -> we keep ~2% for fuel & losses -> ~98% delivered`

*The retained percentage is small -- but across millions of units, getting it right matters.*

> **Notes:** Retainage/retention is the sliver of gas we keep from each shipper, stated as a percentage in our tariff. It's paid in gas (in-kind), not cash. The 2% is illustrative; our actual rate is in the tariff.

---

### Slide 5 -- Why we keep it
- **Compressor fuel:** The engines that push gas through the system run on gas.
- **Lost & unaccounted-for:** Small losses from measurement differences and minor leaks are unavoidable.
- **In-kind reimbursement:** Shippers cover these real costs with gas rather than cash.

> **Notes:** Three reasons retainage exists. Nothing here is optional or unusual -- it's the physical reality of operating a pipeline.

---

### Slide 6 -- A cost to recover, not a margin to earn
- **Keep MORE than we use ->** we over-recovered. The surplus is not profit -- it is gas we owe back to shippers.
- **Keep LESS than we use ->** we under-recovered. We absorbed a cost we may be allowed to collect later.

*Balanced out over time, fuel should cost us nothing and earn us nothing. That's the whole game.*

> **Notes:** The symmetry is the point. Over-recovery is a liability we owe back; under-recovery is a cost we may recover. Neither is profit or loss. If fuel ever shows up as earnings, something is off.

---

### Slide 7 -- The rules, in plain terms
Our regulator (FERC) sets the boundaries. Three of them drive everything.
- **Fair rates:** Our rates must be fair and reasonable -- and on file with the regulator before we charge them.
- **Never over-collect:** If we adjust our rate to track cost, we can never keep a surplus. Everything extra goes back.
- **Show our work:** We report what we retained versus what we actually used.

> **Notes:** Plain-language version of the regulatory framework. We don't need the citations today -- just the principles.

---

### Slide 8 -- Our job, in two steps
- **Step 1 -- Estimate:** Early in the period, estimate the fuel we used and the gas we retained -- so the books are timely and complete, like a flash report.
- **Step 2 -- True up:** When the measured, physical numbers arrive, replace the estimate with reality and record the difference we over- or under-kept.

*Estimate first for speed. Correct to actuals for truth.*

> **Notes:** The core process, tool-agnostic. Estimate so the period isn't blank; then replace with measured actuals and book the difference.

---

### Slide 9 -- Over- or under-recovered: a quick example
- **20,000** -- units we retained from shippers
- **19,200** -- units we actually used & lost
- **800** -- units over, owed back

*Those 800 units aren't profit. They're a surplus we owe back to shippers.*

> **Notes:** A concrete number. If we'd used more than we kept, it would flip to an under-recovery.

---

### Slide 10 -- Where it lands on the books
- **Over-recovered -> a liability.** The gas we owe back sits on the balance sheet until we return it.
- **Under-recovered -> an asset** (when recovery is likely). A cost we expect to collect later.

*Either way, fuel should never create a profit or a loss in our results.*

> **Notes:** This is rate-regulated accounting (ASC 980) at a principle level. The takeaway: these are balance-sheet items, and fuel nets to zero in earnings.

---

### Slide 11 -- Closing the loop: managing the rate
Measuring the gap isn't enough -- we move the rate so the gap trends to zero.
- **Reset the rate:** Adjust the go-forward percentage so what we keep ~ what we actually use.
- **Return or collect the balance:** Give a surplus back to shippers -- or recover a shortfall -- through the rate over time.

*This is the step that keeps shipper retainage from drifting over- or under-accrued.*

> **Notes:** The step teams most often miss. Without this loop, retainage drifts and surpluses build.

---

### Slide 12 -- What "good" looks like
- What we keep is about equal to what we actually use, over a reasonable period.
- The running balance trends toward zero -- not steadily growing.
- Estimates get corrected to measured actuals every period.
- No surplus is left sitting and quietly growing.
- Fuel nets to zero in earnings -- no profit, no loss.

> **Notes:** A simple scorecard the team can self-check against every period.

---

### Slide 13 -- Why your work matters
Get this right and we stay compliant, our shippers are charged fairly, and fuel never distorts our results. Let a surplus build unnoticed, and it becomes exactly what regulators and shippers challenge.

**You are the control that keeps it honest.**

> **Notes:** Make it personal. The team is the control that keeps fuel revenue-neutral and defensible.

---

### Slide 14 -- Key terms
- **Retainage / retention:** the small % of gas we keep from each shipper to cover fuel and losses.
- **Lost & unaccounted-for:** unavoidable small losses from measurement and leaks when moving gas.
- **Over- / under-recovery:** keeping more (or less) gas than we actually used and lost.
- **True-up:** correcting our estimates to the measured, physical actuals.
- **Regulatory asset / liability:** amounts we expect to collect / owe back, held on the balance sheet.

> **Notes:** Keep it handy -- these five terms cover most of what the team will hear.

---

### Slide 15 -- The one thing to remember
> **Set the rate to break even on fuel -- never to profit from it.**
> Everything else is detail.
